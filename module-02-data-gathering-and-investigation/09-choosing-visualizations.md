# Choosing Visualizations

Once we have gathered and explored our data, we might want to present it visually. After all, looking at a huge table of numbers isn't always the easiest way to understand what's happening.

That's where **data visualization** comes in. It means representing data visually using charts, graphs, and other visual elements so that patterns, comparisons, and relationships are easier to understand.

But here's the thing: not every chart works for every situation. The best choice depends on the type of data we have and what we want to find out.

## Common types of visualizations

### 1. Bar charts

A **bar chart** uses rectangular bars to compare values across different categories.

For example, imagine comparing the number of students enrolled in BCA, BCom, and BA. A bar chart would make it easy to see which course has the highest and lowest enrolment.

**Use it when:** You want to compare values between categories.

### 2. Ordered bar charts

An **ordered bar chart** displays categories in a meaningful order, often from the highest value to the lowest.

For example, if customers rate a service as poor, average, good, or excellent, arranging the bars in the appropriate order can make the results easier to interpret.

**Use it when:** You want to compare categories and their order matters.

### 3. Line charts

A **line chart** connects data points with lines to show how a value changes over time.

For example, a business might track its monthly sales to see whether sales are increasing or decreasing.

**Use it when:** You want to identify trends over time.

### 4. Scatter plots

A **scatter plot** uses individual points to show the relationship between two numerical variables.

For example, you could compare the number of hours students study with their exam marks to see whether there appears to be a relationship between them.

**Use it when:** You want to explore a possible relationship between two numerical variables.

One important reminder: just because two variables appear related doesn't mean one causes the other. **Correlation does not prove causation.**

### 5. Histograms

A **histogram** shows how numerical data is distributed across intervals, also called bins.

For example, a teacher could use a histogram to understand how students' exam marks are distributed — whether most students scored around the middle, at the lower end, or at the higher end.

Unlike a typical bar chart, a histogram groups numerical values into intervals rather than comparing separate categories.

**Use it when:** You want to understand the distribution of numerical data.

## Quick comparison

| Visualization | Main purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Bar chart | Compare categories | Students in different courses |
| Ordered bar chart | Compare categories in a meaningful order | Customer satisfaction levels |
| Line chart | Show changes over time | Monthly sales |
| Scatter plot | Explore relationships between numerical variables | Study hours and exam marks |
| Histogram | Show the distribution of numerical data | Distribution of exam scores |

## How do you choose the right chart?

Before creating a chart, ask yourself:

- What question am I trying to answer?
- Am I comparing categories, tracking a trend, exploring a relationship, or studying a distribution?
- What type of variables am I working with?
- Will the chart make the information easier to understand?

The goal isn't to create the prettiest chart. It's to choose a visualization that communicates the information clearly and doesn't mislead the reader.

## My main takeaway

Data visualization helps us make sense of data by presenting it in a visual form. Different charts serve different purposes, so choosing the right one depends on the question being investigated and the type of data available.
